Topic 5 — AI Ethics

A guided reading on how AI systems should be developed, deployed, and used — from the shift toward a human-centered Fifth Industrial Revolution, to four common ethical risks of AI, to eight principles from UNESCO's Recommendation on the Ethics of AI, to applying them in real decisions where humans remain responsible and accountable.

00 · Introduction

Can vs. Should

Artificial Intelligence (AI) increasingly influences how people communicate, learn, work, create, and make decisions.

Generative AI has accelerated this transformation by making systems capable of producing text, images, audio, video, and computer code widely accessible.

These capabilities raise an important ethical question:

Just because AI can do something, does that mean it should?

AI ethics examines how AI systems should be developed, deployed, and used in ways consistent with human values, rights, responsibilities, and well-being.

Why This Matters to Information Systems Students and Professionals

AI is increasingly integrated into organizational information systems, including business applications, analytics, decision-support systems, and automated workflows.

IS professionals may not directly develop AI models, but they often:

Select→ Implement→ Integrate→ Manage→ Evaluate

…AI-enabled systems. Their decisions can affect organizations, users, and other stakeholders.

Responsible IS practice — requires understanding not only what AI can do, but also when and how it should be used.
01 · Understanding AI and Its Ethical Context

From the Fourth to the Fifth Industrial Revolution

The rise of AI can be understood within the broader transformation associated with the Fourth Industrial Revolution (4IR).

The 4IR is characterized by the convergence of digital, physical, and biological technologies, including artificial intelligence, robotics, big data, cloud computing, and the Internet of Things. Its central emphasis is on automation and optimization, using intelligent and interconnected technologies to improve efficiency, productivity, and decision-making.

However, technological progress raises questions beyond what can be automated or optimized. Greater efficiency does not necessarily translate into better outcomes for people, society, or the environment.

Discussions surrounding the emerging Fifth Industrial Revolution (5IR) emphasize a more human-centered, sustainable, and socially responsible approach to technological development. Rather than focusing primarily on technological capabilities, this perspective considers how technology can augment human capabilities and contribute to societal well-being.

The distinction can be summarized as follows:

Fourth Industrial Revolution (4IR)Fifth Industrial Revolution (5IR)
Primary emphasisAutomation and optimizationHuman-centeredness and sustainability
Role of AIImproving efficiency, productivity, and automationAugmenting human capabilities and supporting societal well-being
Central questionHow can technology automate and optimize processes?How can technology serve humanity responsibly and sustainably?

This distinction provides an important foundation for AI ethics. Technological progress should not be evaluated solely by what AI can accomplish, but also by its implications for human autonomy, social well-being, and the environment.

The central question shifts

From
  • What can AI do?
To
  • What should AI do, for whom, and toward what kind of society?

02 · Understanding AI and Its Ethical Context

Understanding AI Before Discussing AI Ethics

Artificial Intelligence

Artificial Intelligence (AI) — refers broadly to computer systems designed to perform tasks associated with human intelligence. Many contemporary AI systems learn patterns from data to classify information, make predictions, recommend actions, or generate outputs.

Their behavior depends partly on the algorithms, data, and design choices underlying them.

Generative Artificial Intelligence

Generative AI (GenAI) — a type of AI designed to create content such as text, images, audio, video, and computer code based on patterns learned from training data.

Large Language Models

Large Language Models (LLMs) — generative AI systems designed to process and generate human-like language.

At a simplified level, LLMs generate text by predicting likely sequences of tokens based on patterns learned during training and the context provided.

Do LLMs Understand Like Humans?

LLMs process language differently from humans. Rather than developing understanding through human experience, they learn statistical patterns and relationships from large datasets, including text from books, websites, and other sources.

These learned relationships enable LLMs to generate coherent responses, answer questions, and perform complex language tasks.

However, producing meaningful language does not necessarily imply understanding it in the same way humans do.

Is AI Sentient?

Sentience — generally refers to the capacity for subjective experience, such as consciously experiencing sensations or feelings.

AI systems can produce responses that resemble human emotions, reasoning, and self-awareness. However, this does not establish that they possess subjective experiences.

Three distinctions are important:

  1. Computation is not consciousness. AI systems process information through mathematical computations, but this does not establish conscious experience.
  2. Simulation is not experience. AI may appear to understand or experience something without necessarily possessing subjective awareness.
  3. Talking about feelings is not feeling. AI can describe emotions without demonstrating that it experiences them.

There is currently no convincing scientific evidence that existing AI systems are sentient.

Why this matters for AI ethics — human-like AI behavior should not automatically be interpreted as evidence of human-like understanding, consciousness, or moral agency.

04 · Understanding AI and Its Ethical Context

Common Ethical Risks of AI

Four common risks illustrate why responsible AI use requires more than technical capability.

Risk

Hallucination

Hallucination occurs when an AI system generates inaccurate, fabricated, or unsupported information that may nevertheless appear convincing.

Example: A student asks an AI chatbot for academic references. The chatbot generates plausible journal citations, but some articles do not exist. The student includes them without verification.

Ethical concern: Hallucination can undermine accuracy, trustworthiness, and professional integrity.

Risk

Bias and Discrimination

AI bias occurs when a system produces systematically skewed or unfair outputs, potentially leading to discriminatory outcomes. Bias may arise from training data, system design, or deployment conditions.

Example: An AI recruitment system trained on historical hiring records favors male applicants because previous hiring practices disproportionately favored men.

Ethical concern: AI may reproduce or amplify existing inequalities, even without deliberate discriminatory intent.

Risk

Data Privacy Issues

Data privacy issues arise when AI systems collect, process, disclose, or use personal and sensitive information without appropriate authorization or safeguards.

Example: A university employee uploads confidential student records to a third-party AI chatbot without verifying whether the information may be shared or how it will be handled.

Ethical concern: Inappropriate AI use can expose sensitive information and violate privacy obligations.

Risk

Overreliance

Overreliance occurs when people depend excessively on AI outputs or recommendations without sufficient independent judgment or verification.

Example: A manager automatically accepts an AI system's recommendations for employee termination without reviewing the evidence or considering relevant circumstances.

Ethical concern: Excessive dependence on AI can weaken human judgment and meaningful oversight.

From Risks to Principles

These risks connect directly to the ethical principles discussed next.

Ethical RiskClosely Related AI Ethics Principle
HallucinationResponsibility and Accountability
Bias and DiscriminationFairness and Non-Discrimination
Data Privacy IssuesRight to Privacy and Data Protection

The connections are not exclusive. A single risk may involve several ethical principles.

Risks and principles work together

05 · Core Ethical Principles for AI

Responsibility and Accountability

About Part II
  • The following eight selected principles are drawn from UNESCO's Recommendation on the Ethics of Artificial Intelligence (2021).

Guide question: What responsibilities do people and institutions have, and who ultimately answers for the consequences?

Responsibility — concerns the duties and obligations associated with AI.
ActorMay be responsible for…
DevelopersTesting systems
OrganizationsEstablishing safeguards
UsersCritically evaluating AI-generated outputs
Accountability — concerns answerability for AI-related decisions, actions, and consequences.

The distinction is straightforward

Responsibility
  • What are people and institutions expected to do?
Accountability
  • Who must answer for what was done and what resulted from it?

For example, when an AI system provides inaccurate information, those who use it in consequential decisions remain responsible for appropriate verification and judgment.

AI assists. Humans remain accountable.

06 · Core Ethical Principles for AI

Human Oversight and Determination

Guide question: Who retains decision-making authority?

Responsible AI requires meaningful human oversight, particularly when systems influence consequential decisions.

Human involvement should include genuine authority to:

Examine· Question· Intervene· Override· Reject

…AI-generated outputs and recommendations.

The rubber-stamp problem. Simply placing a person in the decision-making process is insufficient if that person automatically approves the system's recommendations.

Human oversight therefore requires preserving autonomy and judgment rather than treating AI outputs as unquestionable authority.

AI should augment human capability, not replace human judgment.

07 · Core Ethical Principles for AI

Awareness and Literacy

Guide question: What knowledge and competence are necessary to exercise that authority responsibly?

AI's accessibility does not guarantee that users understand its capabilities, limitations, or implications.

AI literacy — involves understanding how AI systems function at an appropriate level, recognizing their limitations, evaluating their outputs, and considering the risks associated with their use.

Responsible AI use requires awareness of:

  • AI capabilities, limitations, and potential failures.
  • Appropriate tool selection and critical evaluation of outputs.
  • Privacy, security, and other ethical risks.
  • Broader societal and environmental implications.

AI literacy is therefore more than knowing how to operate an AI tool. It involves exercising informed and critical judgment.

Convenience without competence creates ethical risk.

08 · Core Ethical Principles for AI

Fairness and Non-Discrimination

Guide question: Are the benefits, risks, and outcomes of AI distributed fairly?

Fairness — concerns equitable treatment and outcomes.
Non-discrimination — requires preventing unjustified disadvantages based on characteristics such as gender, ethnicity, disability, or socioeconomic status.

Fairness therefore involves more than simply eliminating biased training data.

AI systems may reproduce biases present in training data or introduced through system design and deployment.

Datasets may overrepresent certain populations while underrepresenting others. For example, LLMs trained predominantly on English-language or Western sources may perform less effectively in other linguistic and cultural contexts.

Fairness requires examining whether AI systems:

  1. Produce discriminatory outcomes
  2. Disproportionately disadvantage particular groups
  3. Reinforce existing inequalities

Responsible AI should promote equitable treatment and consider the needs of diverse populations.

09 · Core Ethical Principles for AI

Transparency and Explainability

Guide question: Can the use and decisions of AI be understood and meaningfully questioned?

Transparency — concerns making relevant information about AI systems and their use accessible, including when AI is involved and what role it plays.
Explainability — concerns whether people can meaningfully understand why an AI system produced a particular result.

For example, a student denied a scholarship based on an AI-generated risk assessment should have a meaningful way to understand and question that decision.

Transparency and explainability support accountability, trust, and the ability to challenge consequential AI-mediated decisions.

10 · Core Ethical Principles for AI

Right to Privacy and Data Protection

Guide question: Is data being collected, shared, and used responsibly?

Privacy — concerns individuals' ability to maintain appropriate control over their personal information.
Data protection — involves the safeguards and practices used to protect that information throughout its collection, processing, storage, and sharing.

These concepts build on the discussion of privacy and data in Topic 3.

AI systems frequently process personal, confidential, or sensitive information.

Responsible AI requires considering whether data collection and processing are:

Authorized· Necessary· Appropriately protected· Consistent with individuals' rights

This includes information submitted to third-party AI services, such as student records, organizational documents, personal communications, or unpublished research.

Remember — Access to information does not automatically establish ethical authority to share it with AI systems.
11 · Core Ethical Principles for AI

Safety and Security

Guide question: What harms could result, and are the safeguards proportionate to the risks?

AI systems should be designed and deployed with safeguards against foreseeable harms, failures, vulnerabilities, and misuse.

ConceptConcerns…
SafetyPreventing unacceptable harm resulting from system errors, unintended behavior, or inappropriate use
SecurityProtecting AI systems, data, and infrastructure against unauthorized access, manipulation, and attacks

Safeguards should reflect the likelihood and severity of potential harm. For example, an AI system recommending entertainment does not require the same level of scrutiny as one supporting medical or employment decisions.

The greater the potential harm, the stronger the safeguards should be.

Sustainability

Guide question: Are the long-term social, economic, and environmental costs of AI justified?

Sustainability — requires evaluating whether AI development and use can support present needs without undermining the well-being of future generations.

AI systems depend on computing infrastructure, electricity, cooling resources, hardware, and human labor.

Their development and use can therefore have consequences that are:

Environmental+ Social+ Economic+ Cultural

Sustainability requires considering whether AI's benefits justify its resource consumption and broader long-term impacts, including effects on labor, inequality, and ecological well-being.

Remember — A technologically effective solution is not necessarily a sustainable one.

13 · Applying AI Ethics

From Principles to Practice

Ethical principles help identify concerns, but they do not automatically determine the correct decision.

Scenario — A university is planning to use AI to identify students at risk of dropping out. Early identification could enable timely academic support, but the system also raises ethical questions.
AI Ethics PrincipleQuestion for the University
Responsibility and AccountabilityWho is responsible for the system and accountable for incorrect predictions?
Human Oversight and DeterminationDo humans review predictions before decisions are made?
Awareness and LiteracyDo staff understand the system's capabilities and limitations?
Fairness and Non-DiscriminationCould predictions unfairly disadvantage particular student groups?
Transparency and ExplainabilityCan students understand and question their classifications?
Right to Privacy and Data ProtectionAre student data collected and processed appropriately?
Safety and SecurityCould incorrect predictions cause harm, and are the system and its data adequately protected?
SustainabilityAre the system's benefits justified by its long-term costs and impacts?

The same AI system may offer significant benefits while raising concerns under several principles. Ethical evaluation therefore requires considering these principles together.

Connecting AI Ethics to the Four Ethical Frameworks

The eight principles can be examined through the four ethical frameworks discussed earlier in the course.

Ethical FrameworkAI Ethics PrinciplesCentral Focus
ConsequentialismSafety and Security; SustainabilityHarms, benefits, risks, and long-term consequences
DeontologyResponsibility and Accountability; Human Oversight and Determination; Right to Privacy and Data ProtectionDuties, rights, autonomy, and obligations
Virtue EthicsAwareness and LiteracyCompetence, prudence, integrity, and critical judgment
Justice and FairnessFairness and Non-Discrimination; Transparency and ExplainabilityEquitable treatment and the ability to understand and challenge decisions

These associations are illustrative rather than exclusive. Each principle may be examined using multiple frameworks.

For example, privacy may be evaluated through deontology because it concerns rights and duties, or through consequentialism because privacy violations can cause harm.

The distinction
  • Ethical risks → What can go wrong with AI?
  • AI ethics principles → What ethical concerns should be examined?
  • Ethical frameworks → How should those concerns be reasoned about and evaluated?

14 · Applying AI Ethics

The Central Ethical Question: Who Remains in Control?

AI ethics ultimately concerns the choices people make about technology.

Increasing AI capability does not eliminate human responsibility. AI systems may support analysis, automate tasks, and assist decisions, but their use must remain subject to appropriate human judgment and oversight.

Three principles summarize this relationship:

  • AI assists. Humans remain accountable.— Responsibility and Accountability
  • AI should augment human capability, not replace human judgment.— Human Oversight and Determination
  • Convenience without competence creates ethical risk.— Awareness and Literacy

The central ethical question

Not simply
  • What can AI do?
But
  • What should AI be allowed to do, what should remain distinctly human, and who remains responsible and accountable for the consequences?
Key takeaway — Technological advancement does not eliminate human responsibility and accountability.
